# Test Development

**Test development** is the disciplined translation of a construct and an
intended score use into a specified, piloted, statistically screened, and
documented measurement procedure. Development and validation are
inseparable: every development stage shapes the evidence that will support
the scores. The full treatment of score interpretations, evidence sources,
and validation arguments is in [Validity](/topics/measurement/validity).

**Canonical reference:** [AERA, APA, & NCME, *Standards for Educational and
Psychological Testing*](/resources#standards-for-educational-and-psychological-testing)
#ref/standards

## Orientation: the Standards

The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* is developed
jointly by the American Educational Research Association, the American
Psychological Association, and the National Council on Measurement in
Education. It addresses professional and technical issues of test
development and use in education, psychology, and employment. Its
foundational part comprises three domains, each with recurring themes:

- **[Validity](/topics/measurement/validity).** Establishing the intended uses
  and interpretations of scores; the samples and settings used in validation;
  and Specific Forms of Validity Evidence.
- **Reliability/precision and errors of measurement.** Specifying what counts
  as a replication of the testing procedure; evaluating
  reliability/precision and generalizability coefficients; the factors that
  affect reliability/precision; and standard errors of measurement.
- **Fairness in testing.** Test design, development, administration, and
  scoring procedures that minimize barriers to valid score interpretation
  for the widest possible range of individuals and relevant subgroups; the
  validity of score interpretations for the intended examinee population;
  accommodations that remove construct-irrelevant barriers; and safeguards
  against inappropriate score interpretations.

The machinery behind the second domain is treated in
[Reliability](/topics/measurement/reliability),
[Internal Consistency and Coefficient Alpha](/topics/measurement/internal-consistency),
and [Classical Test Theory](/topics/measurement/classical-test-theory).

## Ten decisions before writing items

Item writing begins only after the instrument's purpose is fixed. Ten
planning decisions precede any item:

1. the purpose for which the test scores will be used;
2. the target population and its demographics — age, gender, race,
   socioeconomic status;
3. the stakes of the use — high-stakes versus low-stakes;
4. the desired level of precision or reliability;
5. whether the instrument must function across cultures;
6. the latent construct or constructs to be measured;
7. the item type;
8. the number of items, or the length of the scale;
9. the content coverage of the item set;
10. the difficulty coverage of the item set.

Decisions 7–10 are elaborated in [Item Development and Response
Formats](/topics/measurement/item-development); decision 4 becomes concrete
evidence through the reliability designs.

## The development pipeline

A complete development effort moves through seven stages, with an empirical
loop at its center:

1. **Fix the purpose and population.** State the intended score use and
   interpretation, and the population the scores will describe.
2. **Define the construct domain.** Establish the attributes and content the
   instrument may legitimately sample.
3. **Write test specifications.** Prepare the blueprint that allocates items
   across the domain.
4. **Draft an exhaustive item pool.** Over-generation is deliberate:
   screening will remove items, so the initial pool should exceed the
   intended final length by a wide margin.
5. **Review the items.** Expert and focus-group review for content,
   clarity, potential bias, and fit to the specifications.
6. **Iterate empirically.** Small-scale pilot with revision or screening of
   items; large-scale pilot on a large, representative sample; formal
   statistical item analysis and screening or revision; reliability and
   validity studies — repeated until the specifications are met.
7. **Norm, set standards, and document.** Norming, standard setting, and
   preparation of the manual for administration, scoring, and
   interpretation of scores.

Stage 6's statistical screening is the subject of [Classical Item
Analysis](/topics/measurement/item-analysis); its reliability studies, of
[Reliability](/topics/measurement/reliability); and its validation evidence,
of [Validity](/topics/measurement/validity).

The loop, not the line, is the point. The earliest standardized instruments
already worked this way: the Stanford–Binet intelligence scales were built
by assembling hundreds of age-graded tasks, trying them on large numbers of
children, retaining only the tasks that discriminated between age levels,
assembling at each age level a cluster of diverse tasks a majority of
children at that age could perform, collecting performance from large
samples, and comparing each child's mental age with chronological age.
Empirical tryout, retention by discrimination, and norming have been part
of standardized measurement since its beginning.

## Test blueprints

A **test blueprint** (test specification) is a two-way plan that crosses
content domains with categories of cognitive demand or difficulty. Its cells
hold planned item counts; its margins summarize how the form's length is
allocated.

| Content domain | Knowledge | Application | Problem solving | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| Domain A | — | — | — | 20 |
| Domain B | — | — | — | 23 |
| Domain C | — | — | — | 19 |
| Domain D | — | — | — | 9 |
| Domain E | — | — | — | 10 |
| Domain F | — | — | — | 19 |
| **Total** | **7** | **62** | **31** | **100** |

(The individual cells of this example are omitted; the margins carry the
allocation.) Reading a blueprint means checking allocations in both
directions. Each row total states how many items its domain contributes;
each column total states how many items target that cognitive category; and
both margins must reconcile with the planned form length — here 100 items. A
blueprint whose margins do not sum to the intended length, or whose cells
are filled opportunistically, documents an aspiration rather than a plan.

Difficulty coverage is sometimes planned as coverage of cognitive operations
rather than as empirical difficulty per se: the blueprint requires items at
each level of a cognitive-demand taxonomy. The substitution is useful but
imperfect, as the caveat below records.

## Job and task analysis

For licensing and certification tests, the content domains themselves are
established empirically through job or task analysis:

1. A panel of subject-matter experts (SMEs) discusses the role of the
   credential in the profession and establishes high-level domains.
2. The SME panel generates a list of job tasks, assigns each task to a
   domain, and reviews the list for duplicates and other defects.
3. The final task list becomes a survey, sent to a representative sample of
   professionals who actually work in the role.
4. Respondents rate each task, usually on its importance and the time spent
   on it — often called *criticality* and *frequency*; demographics are
   gathered alongside.
5. A psychometrician analyzes the ratings to identify the critical and
   frequent tasks.
6. A draft blueprint is recommended, giving top priority to the tasks and
   domains with the greatest importance and frequency.

Step 6 states the decision rule of the whole procedure: blueprint weight
follows importance and frequency ratings from incumbents, not panel opinion
alone.

## Cognitive-demand taxonomies

The column categories of a blueprint are usually drawn from a taxonomy of
cognitive demand. The Bloom taxonomy is the standard reference, in two
versions — the original categories and the revised, verb-based levels:

| Revised level | Original category | Cognitive question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remembering | Knowledge | Can the examinee recall the information? |
| Understanding | Comprehension | Can the examinee explain ideas or concepts? |
| Applying | Application | Can the examinee use the information in a new situation? |
| Analyzing | Analysis | Can the examinee distinguish the parts and their relations? |
| Evaluating | Evaluation | Can the examinee justify a judgment or decision? |
| Creating | Synthesis | Can the examinee produce something new? |

The revised version renames the categories as verbs and moves the top two
levels: creating stands above evaluating, whereas the original placed
evaluation above synthesis. Two cautions govern the use of any such
taxonomy. First, the levels classify *intended cognitive demand*, not
empirical difficulty: an item's taxonomy level says what kind of processing
it calls for, while its difficulty is an empirical property of the encounter
between item and population, estimated during item analysis. Second, the
levels' boundaries are not sharp — the verb lists traditionally attached to
them overlap across categories — so item specifications should state the
convention used to assign an item to a level.

## Assumptions, scope, and failure modes

The pipeline assumes that the construct domain can be specified in advance,
that pilot samples represent the intended population, and that screening
criteria — discrimination targets, reliability goals — follow from the
intended use rather than from convention. Characteristic failure modes:

- **Blueprint drift.** Items written outside the cell they were commissioned
  for, discovered at review or not at all.
- **Cognitive labels mistaken for difficulty.** A difficulty-coverage plan
  filled with taxonomy levels and never checked against empirical item
  difficulty.
- **Unrepresentative pilots.** Stage-6 conclusions drawn from convenience
  samples that do not resemble the intended population.
- **Panel opinion without incumbent data.** Blueprint weights fixed by SMEs
  alone, without the task-analysis survey.
- **Skipped documentation.** A technically sound instrument without a manual
  for administration, scoring, and interpretation fails the very use it was
  built for.
