# Item Development and Response Formats

**Item development** turns the cells of a test blueprint into concrete,
reviewable items. Two requirements govern it from the start: the item format
must be feasible for the intended population, and item writers must work
from explicit item specifications, with training where needed. Format is a
design decision with consequences for scoring and analysis, not an
afterthought to content.

## Feasibility and writer preparation

- **Format feasibility.** Reading level, response mode, and administration
  conditions must suit the population. What a young child, an examinee
  working under time pressure, or a remote respondent can produce constrains
  the admissible formats before content is considered at all.
- **Item specifications and training.** Writers should receive the
  specification each item must fill — content domain, cognitive demand,
  format, and scoring key — and training in the conventions of that format.
  Untrained writers drift toward the formats they personally prefer.

## Optimal performance and typical behavior

Format choice starts from what the instrument asks of the respondent:

- **Optimal (maximum) performance tests** ask respondents to do as well as
  possible; scores estimate capability — aptitude, achievement, knowledge.
  There is a best answer, and the response process is effortful.
- **Typical-behavior and attitude measures** ask respondents to describe how
  they usually feel or act; there is no best answer, and the score describes
  the person rather than ranking performance.

The two families differ in their admissible formats, in their susceptibility
to response bias, and in their scoring logic ([Item and Scale
Scoring](/topics/measurement/scoring); [Response Bias and Careless
Responding](/topics/measurement/response-bias)).

## Selected-response formats for optimal performance

- **Alternate choice.** Exactly two response options, such as true/false or
  yes/no.
- **Multiple choice.** A stem plus one keyed (correct) response and one or
  more incorrect options (distractors); scoring ranges from all-or-nothing
  to partial credit.
- **Matching.** Premises (stimuli), each paired with a response drawn from a
  separate response list; the instructions state whether a response option
  may be used once, more than once, or not at all.

## The master distinction: selected versus constructed response

- **Selected-response items** present the options; the respondent chooses.
  Beyond the three formats above, forced-choice formats — typical of
  behavioral inventories — ask the respondent to select, from a set of
  statements, the one *most* and the one *least* characteristic of
  themselves.
- **Constructed-response items** have the respondent generate the answer:
  essay and short-answer items, graphical constructions, and performance
  tasks.

Selected-response items score cheaply and reliably and sample content
broadly, but cap the kinds of thinking that can be observed;
constructed-response items expose process and production, but carry their
scores through rater judgment, with the agreement problems that implies
([Rater Agreement and Intraclass
Correlation](/topics/measurement/rater-agreement)).

## Formats outside the dichotomy

Several response formats are neither cleanly selected nor cleanly
constructed:

- **Numerical and continuous multistep responses**, including physiological
  and physical measures — EEG, response or reaction time, eye gaze or
  attention time, length — and interactive modes such as a move-along
  cursor. The response is a point or trajectory on a continuum rather than a
  choice among options.
- **Ranking**, including partial ranking, in which only some of the options
  are ordered.
- **Multiple-mark (pick *k* of *N*) items**, in which the respondent selects
  a subset of options. Variants supply *k* or leave it open, and some ask
  that the selected options then be rank-ordered. Scoring algorithms for
  multiple-mark items differ materially in how they treat errors of
  omission and commission; they are compared in [Item and Scale
  Scoring](/topics/measurement/scoring).
- **Checklists**, in which each entry is endorsed or not — observed
  behaviors, symptoms, characteristics.
- **Sorting**, in which items are assigned to categories or piles defined by
  the designer.

## The Q-sort technique

A **Q-sort** is a sorting format with a forced distribution: the evaluator
orders a set of statements (Q-items) into a designated number of categories,
running from the statements *most characteristic* — most salient in
describing the person — at one end to the *most uncharacteristic* or least
salient at the other, with a pre-assigned number of items placed in each
category.

Three design decisions shape what the resulting sort can support:

- **Number of piles.** Designs commonly use 7 or 9 categories.
- **Distribution shape.** Uniform — equal counts in every pile — or
  approximately normal, with more items in the middle piles.
- **Placement policy.** Whether every statement must be placed, or
  statements that are inapplicable or unobserved may be left out.

The analysis of Q-sort data — item, subscale, and criterion-sort scoring —
is treated in [Item and Scale Scoring](/topics/measurement/scoring).

## Item-writing failure rules

Candidate items fail in recurring, recognizable ways. Seven failure patterns
serve as a review checklist; each is stated here as a general rule.

1. **Sensitive or self-incriminating content.** Items that ask respondents
   to admit abuse, dishonesty, or illegal behavior invite defensive
   distortion rather than honest reporting. Where such content is
   unavoidable, anonymity and privacy-preserving designs ([Randomized
   Response Techniques](/topics/measurement/randomized-response)) reduce the
   pressure to distort.
2. **Double-barreled content.** An item that asks about two behaviors at
   once — physically *or* verbally doing something — cannot be answered
   accurately by respondents for whom the two halves differ. Split it into
   separate items.
3. **Ambiguous terms and units.** Everyday words with technical readings —
   a "lap" in a pool of known length — change the correct answer depending
   on which interpretation the respondent adopts. Define terms and units in
   the stem.
4. **Leading or loaded premises.** A stem that embeds its own justification
   ("...because this way only the deserving would benefit") or presupposes
   agreement ("now that you have seen the advantages of...") measures
   reaction to the frame, not the target attitude. State the proposition
   neutrally.
5. **Neutral versus "don't know."** A no-lean midpoint and a "don't know"
   option answer different questions — indifference versus unfamiliarity.
   Offering one, both, or neither changes the meaning of every other
   option; the choice must be deliberate.
6. **Negative wording.** Negatively framed stems ("spending should not be
   reduced") require respondents to reverse the proposition before agreeing
   or disagreeing, and disagreement becomes a double negation. Prefer
   positive framing; where negation is necessary, typographic emphasis
   reduces misreading.
7. **Mutually exclusive, exhaustive response categories.** Categories must
   not overlap — age brackets "10–20" and "20–30" both contain 20 — and
   must cover the full range, including an open upper bracket. Nominal
   categories such as ethnicity need inclusive residual options, for
   example multiracial and other.

Anatomical flaws are cheapest to fix at the review stage; statistical
screening can only catch the flaws that survive into empirical data
([Classical Item Analysis](/topics/measurement/item-analysis)).

## Assumptions, scope, and failure modes

Item development as practiced here assumes format feasibility for the
population and specification-driven writing. Its characteristic failure
modes:

- **Format–construct mismatch.** Binary checklists for high-inference
  constructs; ranking or forced formats where the information of individual
  items matters downstream.
- **Forced-format tradeoffs.** Q-sorts and forced-choice items buy
  descriptive precision and comparability of placements at the cost of
  constraints on how the data may be scored and compared; the constraints
  are treated in [Item and Scale Scoring](/topics/measurement/scoring).
- **Category defects.** Overlapping or non-exhaustive options bias every
  response they touch, before any statistical analysis can notice.
- **Writer drift.** Items that stray from the blueprint cell they were
  commissioned to fill, undermining the allocation the blueprint specifies.
