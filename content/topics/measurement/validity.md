# Validity

**Validity** is the degree to which evidence and theory support the
interpretation of test scores for a proposed use of a test. What is evaluated
is the *interpretation of the scores for that use*, not the test itself: the
same instrument can support one interpretation and fail to support another, so
statements about validity refer to particular interpretations for specified
uses, and the unqualified phrase "the validity of the test" is incorrect.
**Validation** is the corresponding process — accumulating relevant evidence to
provide a sound scientific basis for the proposed score interpretations.

**Canonical reference:** [AERA, APA, & NCME, *Standards for Educational and
Psychological Testing*](/resources#standards-for-educational-and-psychological-testing)
#ref/standards

## The interpretation–use–population statement

Validation logically begins with an explicit statement of the proposed
interpretation of the scores, together with a rationale for the relevance of
that interpretation to the proposed use. A complete statement fixes three
things:

- **Interpretation.** What the scores are claimed to mean — the *construct*,
  the concept or characteristic the test is designed to measure, together with
  the score level or pattern that carries the claim.
- **Use.** What decisions or actions the scores will inform — placement,
  selection, diagnosis, licensure, program evaluation.
- **Population.** The individuals about whom scores will be interpreted, with
  the contexts of testing delimited.

The proposed interpretation is elaborated through a **conceptual framework**
for the test: a description of the construct's scope and extent, the knowledge,
skills, traits, or processes to be assessed, and — ideally — how the construct
is to be distinguished from neighboring constructs and how it should relate to
other variables. The framework is shaped by the intended uses. A mathematics
achievement test used for course placement, for endorsing a diploma, or for
admissions implies three somewhat different interpretations of the same scores,
and each use reshapes the framework and the evidence needed.

When scores are interpreted in more than one way, each intended interpretation
must be validated separately. Evidence supporting an interpretation for one
use — placement, say — does not transfer to other uses such as promotion
decisions or teacher evaluation. A usable template:

> Scores on this instrument are interpreted as indicators of *construct* for
> individuals in *population*, administered under *conditions*, for the purpose
> of *use*.

Sufficient validity evidence is a necessary condition for justifiable test
use, not a sufficient one: the decision to administer a test also weighs cost
against benefit and negative against positive consequences. Those weighings
accompany the validity argument; they do not replace it.

## Validation as an accumulating argument

Validation can be viewed as constructing and evaluating arguments *for and
against* the intended interpretation of test scores and its relevance to the
proposed use. The argument is built from **propositions** — testable claims
that, together, would support the interpretation. For a mathematics test used
to assess readiness for an advanced course, relevant propositions include:

1. certain skills are in fact prerequisite for the course;
2. the test's content domain is consistent with those prerequisite skills;
3. scores generalize across relevant sets of items;
4. scores are not unduly influenced by ancillary variables such as writing
   ability;
5. success in the course can itself be validly assessed; and
6. test takers with high scores will fare better in the course than test
   takers with low scores.

Once the propositions are articulated, validation proceeds by obtaining
empirical evidence, examining relevant literature, and conducting logical
analyses to evaluate each one. The argument evolves: as evidence accumulates
about what can and cannot be inferred from the scores, the test, the
conceptual framework, and even the construct itself may need revision — and
interpretations drawn from a revised test require validation anew.

**Rival hypotheses** sharpen the proposition list. Most rivals allege that the
test measures less than its proposed construct or more than it: these are
construct underrepresentation and construct-irrelevant variance, the two
threats described next. A finding of unintended consequences can also prompt
rival hypotheses.

Two norms govern the argument's quality. First, a few pieces of solid
evidence bearing on a proposition are better than numerous pieces of
questionable evidence. Second, the conclusion is a judgment that the
*preponderance* of available evidence supports the interpretation — reached by
professional judgment, not by a single statistic. An interpretation may fail
either for lack of supporting evidence or because credible evidence weighs
against it. Higher stakes entail higher standards of evidence, and where data
collection is costly, interpretations may perforce rest on fewer data. In this
sense validation never ends, though it reaches provisional closure once the
summary judgment is well supported and defensible.

## Two threats to score meaning: underrepresentation and irrelevant variance

**Construct underrepresentation** (or construct deficiency) is the degree to
which a test fails to capture important aspects of the construct. It narrows
the meaning of scores: the test does not adequately sample some content,
engage some psychological processes, or elicit some ways of responding that
the intended construct encompasses. A test intended as a comprehensive measure
of anxiety that captures only physiological reactions underrepresents the
construct by omitting emotional, cognitive, and situational components; a
reading comprehension test with too narrow a range of passage types, or that
ignores a common kind of reading material, underrepresents in the same way.

**Construct-irrelevant variance** (or construct contamination) is the degree
to which scores are affected by processes extraneous to the intended purpose.
On a reading comprehension test, irrelevant sources include passage material
far above or below the targeted difficulty, emotional reactions to passage
content, familiarity with the passages' subject matter, and the writing skill
needed to compose answers. On a mathematics test, overreliance on reading
comprehension may disadvantage English language learners. A tendency to
underreport anxiety is a source of irrelevant variance on an anxiety measure —
one of the systematic tendencies treated under [Response Bias and Careless
Responding](/topics/measurement/response-bias). Whether a component counts as
irrelevant depends on the construct's detailed definition: vocabulary
knowledge or reading speed may or may not belong to the construct a given test
targets.

Nearly every test leaves out something some users believe should be measured
and includes something others consider inappropriate. Validation attends to
both distortions, and to aspects of format, administration conditions, and
language level that may limit or qualify score interpretation for particular
groups of test takers — the point at which validity and fairness converge.

## Sources of validity evidence

Five sources of evidence illuminate different aspects of validity, but they do
not represent distinct *types* of validity. Validity is a unitary concept: the
degree to which all the accumulated evidence supports the intended
interpretation for the proposed use. The *Standards* deliberately avoids the
historical labels *content validity* and *predictive validity*: under the
unitary view, the former "types" are reorganized as evidence sources that
jointly support a single construct-based argument. No source is required in
every setting, and none is inherently
preferable; the quality and relevance of evidence to the propositions at issue
determine its value. A proposition that a test predicts a criterion can be
supported without evidence about content sampling, and a proposition about
content coverage can be supported without predictor–criterion evidence; a
proposition set invoking both requires both. Adequate support for proposed
interpretations usually requires multiple sources.

| Source | Central question | Typical study procedures | Principal limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Test content | Do the themes, wording, and format of items represent the construct domain, and is that domain right for the proposed inference? | Content specifications; experts assign items to domain categories and judge representativeness; job/task analysis feeding job-sample tests; alignment studies | Judgment-dependent; panel expertise and diversity decide quality; a domain apt for one inference may be wrong for another |
| Response processes | Do test takers (and scorers) actually engage the processes the construct and scoring rationale presume? | Interviews and questioning about strategies; records of successive drafts or electronically monitored revisions; response times and eye movements; studies of observer and judge processes | Only required when process claims are part of the argument; verbal reports cover what test takers can articulate; study conditions may differ from operational ones |
| Internal structure | Do relationships among items and test components conform to the structure the construct implies? | Factor analysis for dimensionality; item homogeneity analyses; conformity to expected difficulty orderings; differential item functioning studies | Says nothing directly about relevance to external uses; a single consistency index is inappropriate for multidimensional tests |
| Relations to other variables | Are score relationships with external measures the theory predicts — and no others — actually observed? | Convergent/discriminant matrices; predictive and concurrent criterion studies; known-groups and intervention studies; multitrait–multimethod designs; differential prediction analyses; meta-analytic generalization | Vulnerable to criterion flaws, range restriction, and attenuation; correlations alone cannot link score levels to outcome levels; single local studies are imprecise |
| Consequences | Do the intended uses yield their claimed benefits, and do unintended outcomes trace to flaws in score meaning or elsewhere? | Monitoring claimed benefits; tracing unintended outcomes (e.g., subgroup selection rates) to underrepresentation, irrelevant variance, or other causes | Causal attribution is difficult; consequences often combine validity-relevant and policy-relevant sources; positive and negative effects co-occur |

### Evidence based on test content

Test content comprises the themes, wording, and format of the items, tasks, or
questions; administration and scoring procedures may also bear on
content-based evidence. Developers work from a **content specification** that
describes the domain in detail, classifying content areas and item types.
Evidence then takes the form of logical or empirical analyses of how adequately
the content represents the domain and how relevant the domain is to the
proposed interpretation. In licensure testing, experts in the occupation assign
test items to categories defined by the facets for which the occupation is
regulated, and judge the representativeness of the resulting item set. In
employment testing, systematic observation of the job domain, combined with
expert judgments of task importance, criticality, and frequency, supports a job
sample built from a random or stratified sampling of highly rated tasks — the
job-analysis machinery described under [Test
Development](/topics/measurement/test-development). In education, content
evidence is the heart of **alignment** studies, which evaluate the
correspondence between learning standards and test content: whether the
content samples the standards' domain, whether item cognitive demands match the
standards' levels, and whether irrelevant features are avoided.

Content evidence also addresses subgroup questions: vocabulary more complex
than the job requires is a source of irrelevant difficulty for some test
takers, and review by a diverse panel of experts can surface sources of
irrelevant difficulty or easiness that warrant further study. The domain's
appropriateness is inference-specific. A domain that properly includes material
little taught in the curriculum suits a research comparison of achievement
across states; a test used for promotion or graduation decisions against a
delivered curriculum is appropriately limited to what students had the
opportunity to learn.

### Evidence based on response processes

Many construct interpretations embed assumptions about the cognitive processes
test takers engage. When the rationale for score interpretation depends on such
premises, theoretical or empirical evidence for them is needed: a test intended
to assess mathematical reasoning requires evidence that test takers are
reasoning rather than applying an algorithm specific to the practice items.
Procedures include questioning test takers from the intended population about
their strategies and responses to particular items; maintaining records that
monitor the development of a response, such as successive drafts or
electronically monitored revisions of writing; and documenting eye movements or
response times where relevant. Process inferences can also be developed
indirectly, from relationships among parts of the test and with other
variables.

Scorers' processes count too. When observers or judges are expected to apply
particular criteria, evidence is needed that they in fact apply those criteria
and are not swayed by irrelevant ones (handwriting quality is irrelevant to
judging an essay's content). Studies of how raters record and evaluate
performance belong here, with the agreement and consistency indices themselves
treated under [Rater Agreement and Intraclass
Correlation](/topics/measurement/rater-agreement).

Process evidence is not always required. Where multiple routes to a correct
solution exist and the construct is concerned only with whether the problem was
solved, claims about response processes are not part of the validity argument.
Wide individual differences in process can nonetheless be revealing and may
lead to reconsidering certain formats.

### Evidence based on internal structure

Analyses of internal structure indicate whether the relationships among items
and components conform to the construct's presumed structure. A framework
implying a single dimension calls for evidence of item homogeneity — a
unidimensionality claim can be supported by factor analysis showing that one
major dimension accounts for most score variability, or that a single factor
adequately accounts for item covariation (the models are developed in [Factor
Models and Reliability](/topics/measurement/factor-analysis)). A framework
positing distinct components — a health survey assessing both physical and
emotional discomfort — calls for evidence that item interrelations bear out the
distinction. A framework positing a hierarchy of increasingly demanding
components calls for evidence that response patterns conform to that ordering.
An internal-consistency coefficient summarizes one specific kind of item
interrelation and would be inappropriate as a structural summary for a test
with a more complex internal structure; those coefficients are treated in
[Internal Consistency and Coefficient Alpha](/topics/measurement/internal-consistency),
while the broader choice of error facets is treated in
[Reliability](/topics/measurement/reliability).

Internal-structure studies also include **differential item functioning**
(DIF): different groups of test takers with similar overall ability, or similar
standing on an appropriate criterion, responding systematically differently to
a particular item on average. DIF is not automatically a flaw: item subsets
sharing specific content or task representation may legitimately function
differently across groups, a multidimensionality that may conform to the test
framework. Its investigation belongs to fairness review as well as to
structure.

### Evidence based on relations to other variables

The intended interpretation usually implies that scores should relate to some
external variables and not others; analyses of those relationships form the
fourth source.

**Convergent and discriminant evidence.** Relationships with measures of the
same or similar constructs provide convergent evidence; relationships with
measures of purportedly different constructs provide discriminant evidence.
Scores on a multiple-choice reading comprehension test should relate closely
to other reading comprehension measures based on other methods, such as essay
tasks, and less closely to measures of logical reasoning. Relationships among
*different methods* of measuring the construct are especially helpful in
sharpening score meaning — the correlation machinery for such studies is
treated under [Correlation](/topics/measurement/correlation).

**Group-membership and experimental evidence.** Categorical variables,
including group membership, become relevant when theory suggests group
differences should be present or absent if the interpretation is sound — the
design often called known-groups evidence. Experimental designs supply stronger
inferences: whether anxiety scores improve following treatment, whether an
achievement test differentiates instructed from noninstructed groups, or — if
short-term coaching is viewed as a validity threat — whether coached and
uncoached groups perform differently.

**Test–criterion designs.** The criterion variable measures an attribute or
outcome operationally distinct from the test; whether the test predicts it is a
testable hypothesis. The fundamental question is always how accurately scores
predict criterion performance, with the required degree of accuracy — and the
score range over which it is needed — fixed by the use. Two classic designs are
distinguished:

- A **predictive** study relates test scores to criterion scores obtained
  later. When prediction is genuinely contemplated, as in admissions or
  employment, it retains the temporal structure and other characteristics of
  the practical decision.
- A **concurrent** study collects test and criterion information at about the
  same time. It avoids temporal change and is particularly useful for
  psychodiagnostic instruments and for comparing alternative measures of a
  construct for which an accepted measurement procedure already exists.

Prior evidence about whether predictive and concurrent studies agree in a
domain usefully informs the choice between them.

**Criterion quality.** The credibility of a test–criterion study depends on
the relevance, reliability, and validity of the *criterion's* interpretation
for the application. A criterion description should include evidence of its
reliability, the extent to which it represents the intended construct (job
performance, course grades, diagnoses), and its susceptibility to extraneous
variance — with special attention to sources previous research suggests may
bias it for or against identifiable groups. For linking specific score levels
to criterion levels, regression equations are more useful than correlations,
which cannot fully describe the pattern of association; means, standard
deviations, and information about the distribution of criterion performance at
given test scores should accompany them, with methods appropriate to
categorical outcomes (logistic regression for dichotomous criteria). Samples
selected for extreme scores on one of the measures typically cannot provide
adequate information about the association — the selection problems treated
under [Range Restriction and Selection
Corrections](/topics/measurement/range-restriction).

**Multiple predictors.** When scores are used with other variables to predict
an outcome, the optimum combination cannot be determined from separate
pairwise examinations of each predictor with the criterion, because predictors
intercorrelate. It is often informative to estimate the increment in accuracy
each variable adds beyond all others; because empirically derived weights
capitalize on chance, multiple-predictor models should be cross-validated
whenever feasible.

**Differential prediction.** Evidence from relations to other variables also
supports — or challenges — comparable score meaning across subgroups. A
finding that the test–criterion relationship differs across groups may
indicate that score meaning differs (via construct underrepresentation or
construct-irrelevant variance in the test), that the *criterion* has different
meaning for different groups, or merely measurement error, especially when
group means differ. One approach is moderated regression of the criterion on
the score, group membership, and their interaction:

$$
Y = b_0 + b_1 X + b_2 G + b_3 (X \times G) + e
$$

For a binary indicator $G$ coded 0 for the reference group and 1 for the
comparison group, $b_0$ and $b_1$ are the reference-group intercept and slope,
and $e$ is the regression residual. A nonzero population interaction $b_3$
means differing slopes; a statistically
reliable sample estimate $\hat b_3$ supplies evidence for that difference. When
the interaction is absent, a nonzero $b_2$ means differing intercepts. With an
interaction present, $b_2$ is instead the group effect at $X=0$, so intercepts
should be compared at a substantively meaningful reference point, often after
centering $X$. The same questions can be examined for several groups through
systematic departures from a common regression line rather than a binary-coded
interaction. None of these outcomes by itself fixes where the problem lies; the
follow-up inquiry traces the difference to test, criterion, or error.

**Classification and placement.** When scores assign people to treatments,
jobs, or programs, prediction of each outcome is not enough: support for the
*classification* procedure requires evidence that the test helps determine who
would profit differentially from one alternative rather than another. A test
can be highly predictive of performance in several programs yet supply no
basis for choosing between them; whenever feasible, evidence of differential
treatment outcomes should be provided, though ethical and legal constraints on
differential assignment can make such research infeasible.

**Validity generalization.** Observed test–criterion correlations vary
substantially across times and places, historically prompting a presumption
that local studies are always required. Meta-analysis — the most widely used
approach — shows that in some domains much of this variability is attributable
to statistical artifacts: sampling fluctuation, variation across studies in
score range, and criterion unreliability. When the meta-analytic database is
large, adequately represents the target situation, and yields a clear,
consistent pattern after artifact correction, the informational value of a
small local study may be limited — even misleading. When the database is
small, inconsistent, or unlike the new situation, local evidence is the more
informative; a single careful, adequately sized study can suffice to support
or reject use in a new setting. Generalization studies vary along situational
facets — how the predictor construct is measured, the type of job or
curriculum, the criterion type, the test takers, and the time period — and a
major objective is estimating empirically how variation in these facets
affects the obtained relationships. Meta-analytic technique extends to other
inferences as well, such as coaching effects and the effects of testing
alterations for test takers with disabilities. Anyone relying on an existing
meta-analysis must judge its soundness for the local setting: the tests,
criteria, and situational features must be comparable, and significant
disparities noted explicitly. Corrections for range restriction and
attenuation rest on assumptions that should be stated, with both adjusted and
unadjusted coefficients reported.

### Evidence based on consequences

Some consequences of test use follow directly from the interpretations and
uses the developer intends; evaluating the soundness of those interpretations
is part of validation. A fundamental purpose of validation is to indicate
whether the anticipated benefits — efficacious therapy selection, suitable job
placement, exclusion of unqualified candidates, improved instruction — are
likely to be realized. A placement test's validation is informed by evidence
that alternative placements are differentially beneficial; a publisher's claim
of reduced training costs calls for evidence in support of that proposition,
and any asserted outcome of a recommended use requires a stated basis plus
relevant evidence, including findings that weigh against it.

Other consequence claims extend *beyond* score meaning. A claim that
achievement data will identify and improve low-performing schools, or that
accountability testing will improve motivation or instructional practice,
rests on propositions about the system or intervention, not about what the
scores measure. Those making such claims are responsible for evaluating them;
where the claims are central to the justification for testing, they become part
of the argument for test use and demand evidence of their own.

The distinction decides when consequences bear on *validity*. Consider a
district screening children for kindergarten readiness: if high scores predict
performance on key kindergarten tasks, the scores are valid for that
prediction. But the further claim that *screening out* low scorers produces
the greatest benefit requires different evidence — perhaps some low-scoring
children would benefit more from kindergarten access than from exclusion. The
developer answers for the readiness interpretation; the district answers for
the placement policy.

Unintended consequences require the same tracing. The key distinction is
between consequences arising from a source of error *in* the intended score
interpretation and consequences arising elsewhere. If an employment test
yields different hiring rates for demographic groups, and the difference
reflects an unequal distribution of the very skills the test measures — skills
that genuinely contribute to job performance — the group difference by itself
does not impugn the interpretation. If the test instead measures skill
differences unrelated to the job (a sophisticated reading test where the job
needs only minimal literacy), or is sensitive to a characteristic outside the
construct, the interpretation of comparable prediction for all applicant
groups is invalidated — as it is when construct underrepresentation (omitting
assessable components on which subgroup differences are smaller) visibly
distorts selection rates. But an unintended consequence need not be a validity
defect at all: applicants who resent a personality inventory as invasive of
privacy may form a negative impression of the employer, a real cost that says
nothing about whether the scores predict performance — an employer may
discontinue the test over it anyway. Unintended consequences can equally be
positive; one test use typically produces several at once, and reasonable
decision makers can weigh them differently. In short, decisions about test use
are appropriately informed by three separable inputs: validity evidence for
the intended interpretation, evidence for additional consequence claims, and
value judgments — alongside legal constraints, which can be determinative and
may limit discarding scores already administered.

When unintended consequences appear, an attempt should be made to investigate
whether they arise from the test's sensitivity to characteristics other than
those intended, or from its failure to represent the construct fully. Group
differences, while not themselves questioning validity, can increase the
salience of plausible rival hypotheses that the validation effort should then
evaluate.

## Samples and settings used in validation

Validation findings inherit the limitations of the samples and conditions that
produced them. A coefficient, a factor pattern, or an expert-rating result is
evidence only for interpretations in populations and conditions it can
plausibly represent — so the samples and settings from which evidence comes
are a validation concern in their own right, not an appendix to it.

**Describing the sample.** The composition of any sample from which validity
evidence is obtained should be described in as much detail as practical and
permissible: major sociodemographic and developmental characteristics, plus
whatever the setting makes diagnostic of the sample's relevance — diagnoses
and their severity for patient samples; applicant versus incumbent status,
experience level, and demographic composition in employment; candidates versus
already-credentialed practitioners in credentialing; educational background,
developmental level, and community characteristics in education. Where the
sample is meant to represent a population, that population should be described
and systematic limits on representativeness — self-selection, attrition,
linguistic ability, disability status, exclusion criteria — drawn to attention,
along with the extent of missing data and how missing data were handled.
Privacy law can limit how much population detail may be disclosed, and the
description should never identify individuals; what can be said, said at what
level of particularity, is itself part of the reporting.

**Describing the conditions.** Statistical findings should carry enough
description of the data collection for a user to judge their relevance to
local conditions: test-taker motivation and prior preparation, the range of
scores in the sample, time allowed, administration mode (proctored on-site
versus unproctored online), examiner training, intervals between measures, and
anything that has changed since the evidence was obtained. Features of a
validity study that differ from operational testing conditions can plausibly
influence performance, and they qualify how far the findings transfer.

**Judges are samples too.** Where validation rests in part on the opinions or
decisions of expert judges, observers, or raters, the procedures for selecting
them and eliciting their judgments belong to the evidence: qualifications and
experience, training and instructions, whether judgments were reached
independently, the level of agreement achieved, and how any exchange of
information among judges proceeded. Consensus procedures are entirely
legitimate, but judgments produced through interaction are not independent
observations and must not be analyzed as such; different judge sets may
properly serve different purposes, such as rating cultural sensitivity on one
hand and reading level on the other.

**Subgroup evidence.** A proposed interpretation normally covers a whole
intended population, so validation samples should contain the relevant
subgroups in adequate numbers, and the consistency of support should be
examined across them — through diverse content review, DIF analysis, and
differential prediction as described above. Evidence that sustains the
interpretation for some subgroups but not others narrows the interpretation
the argument can support and points toward the fairness responses of test
modification, accommodation, or restriction of use. Because subgroup
comparisons need adequate numbers to estimate precisely, sample-size
planning belongs to validation design, not just to the analysis stage.

**Local and accumulated evidence.** Empirical evidence may be *local*,
produced within the contexts where the test will be used, or *accumulated*
from similar testing applications in other settings; studies conducted
locally, specific prior studies, and statistical syntheses of studies meeting
clearly stated quality criteria are all legitimate strands of the argument.
Existing evidence from similar tests and contexts strengthens the argument
precisely where local data are limited — while small samples make local
estimates imprecise, accumulated evidence carries only when its samples,
criteria, and conditions resemble the local situation. That comparison, and
the decision of how much local study a given use requires, is the judgment
developed in the validity-generalization discussion above. What must be
gathered — locally or otherwise — is fixed by the propositions underlying the
interpretation for the use, not by a standing requirement to collect every
source of evidence in every setting.

## Integrating the evidence

A sound **validity argument** integrates the various strands into a coherent
account of the degree to which existing evidence and theory support the
intended interpretation for specific uses, combining new studies with
previously reported research. Because an interpretation typically rests on
more than one proposition, strong support for one part never diminishes the
need for evidence on the others: a strong predictor–criterion relationship in
employment selection is ordinarily insufficient by itself, without evidence on
the criterion's appropriateness, on the suitability of materials and
procedures for the full range of applicants, and on the consistency of
support across groups.

The argument may indicate the need to refine the construct's definition, to
revise the test or its administration, or to gather further evidence. It
rests, ultimately, on *all* available evidence relevant to the technical
quality of the testing system: careful test construction; adequate score
reliability; appropriate administration and scoring; accurate scaling,
equating, and standard setting; and careful attention to fairness — with the
weight of evidence required rising with the stakes of the use.

## Responsibilities of developers and users

Validation is the joint responsibility of the test developer and the test
user.

- The **developer** sets forth clearly how scores are intended to be
  interpreted and used, delimits the intended population, and describes the
  construct; furnishes the rationale and a summary of evidence and theory for
  each intended interpretation; and provides specific warnings where
  experience suggests a test is likely to be misused for certain decisions or
  certain test takers. Where a common or likely interpretation has not been
  evaluated, or is inconsistent with available evidence, that fact should be
  made clear.
- The **user** bears ultimate responsibility for evaluating the quality and
  local relevance of the validity evidence in the setting of use. When a user
  proposes an interpretation or use the developer has not supported, the
  responsibility for providing validity evidence shifts to that user — and
  when a test is altered, or administered in a way that changes the underlying
  construct for some subgroup, the modified test needs its own evidence for
  the intended interpretation.
- Claims of indirect benefit from a testing program carry their own
  evidentiary duties: the recommender must make the rationale explicit and
  supply logical or empirical support, giving due weight to contradictory
  findings.
- Interpretation materials should enable users to judge the confidence each
  recommended interpretation warrants, and should discourage overinterpretation
  of isolated items, small item subsets, or individual subscores.

Other researchers' findings about what scores mean contribute to the evidence
base; validation is not confined to the developer's own studies.

## Relation to reliability and fairness

Reliability/precision is distinct from validity, but it bounds it. To the
extent scores are inconsistent across replications of the testing procedure,
their potential for accurate criterion prediction, beneficial diagnosis, and
sound decisions is limited. Under classical test theory's uncorrelated-error
assumptions, random measurement error in either variable attenuates their
observed relationship: the population correlation between two fallible
measures cannot exceed the geometric mean of their reliabilities,

$$
|\rho_{XY}| \le \sqrt{\rho_{XX'}\, \rho_{YY'}},
$$

so an unreliable test or an unreliable criterion caps the validity coefficient
a study can show, and corrections for attenuation — removing the estimated
effect of measurement error — yield *adjusted* estimates that must be reported
as such, with the method and statistics used (see [Classical Test
Theory](/topics/measurement/classical-test-theory)). The reliability designs
and error facets themselves are treated under
[Reliability](/topics/measurement/reliability).

Fairness intersects validity through the two construct threats. Construct
underrepresentation and construct-irrelevant variance can give one subgroup an
unfair advantage or disadvantage, turning a validity question into a fairness
question; accommodations that remove construct-irrelevant barriers preserve
score meaning, but adaptations that change the construct being measured
require fresh validity evidence for the modified test. Fair review also
safeguards against inappropriate score interpretations for subgroups, and DIF
analysis links the internal-structure evidence above to subgroup review.

## A practical validation workflow

1. **State the interpretation, use, and population.** Write the explicit
   statement; fix the construct and the rationale linking interpretation to
   use.
2. **Elaborate the conceptual framework.** Describe the construct's scope,
   distinguish it from neighboring constructs, and state expected relations to
   other variables.
3. **Derive propositions and rival hypotheses.** List the claims that would
   support the interpretation for this use; generate rivals via
   underrepresentation and irrelevant variance; add propositions about
   anticipated consequences.
4. **Map propositions to evidence sources**, deciding which require local
   study and which can lean on existing evidence or meta-analysis, and
   checking that the samples and settings behind any accumulated evidence
   match the local use; scale the effort to the stakes of the use.
5. **Gather evidence.** Convene qualified and diverse content panels; run
   process studies where the rationale presumes processes; analyze internal
   structure; design external-relations studies (choosing predictive or
   concurrent designs, developing and auditing the criterion, planning subgroup
   analyses); begin monitoring consequences at first operational use.
6. **Integrate and judge.** Weigh evidence by quality and relevance, give due
   weight to inconsistent findings, and reach the preponderance judgment;
   identify needed revisions to test, framework, or construct — and revalidate
   whatever changes.
7. **Document and monitor.** Publish the rationale with the summary of
   evidence, describe samples and conditions, warn against unsupported
   interpretations, and schedule re-examination as populations or
   alternatives change.

| Proposition to support | Primary evidence sources |
| --- | --- |
| Scores cover the defined domain | Test content |
| Items engage the intended cognitive or scoring processes | Response processes |
| Score relationships match the construct's structure | Internal structure |
| Scores relate to what they should — criteria, similar constructs — and not to what they should not | Relations to other variables |
| The use yields its claimed benefits | Consequences (with relations for benefit mechanisms) |
| Scores function comparably across subgroups | Content review, internal structure (DIF), relations (differential prediction) |

## Reporting checklist

A validation report supports independent evaluation when it includes:

- **Interpretations and rationale.** Each intended interpretation for each
  use; the delimited population and described construct; the propositions
  investigated; the rationale and a summary of evidence and theory, giving due
  weight to findings inconsistent with the intended interpretation; explicit
  warnings for likely but unsupported interpretations; stated bases for any
  claimed outcome of test use, including indirect benefits and insensitivity
  to practice or coaching.
- **Samples and settings.** Sample composition in practical detail —
  demographics, developmental characteristics, self-selection, attrition,
  linguistic ability, disability status, exclusion criteria, and the
  population a sample is meant to represent; the conditions of data collection
  (motivation, preparation, score range, timing, administration mode,
  examiner training, intervals between measures) and any departures from
  operational conditions.
- **Expert judgment procedures.** How judges, observers, and raters were
  selected and trained; their qualifications; whether judgments were
  independent; and agreement levels with the agreement criterion defined
  (exact or within k scale points, before or after consensus discussion).
- **Content evidence.** The procedures for specifying and generating content,
  justified against the intended population and construct; the mapping of
  items to the domain, including domain areas not covered; any importance,
  frequency, or criticality criteria explained.
- **Process evidence.** Support for premises about test-taker cognitive
  processes and about observer/scorer processes, wherever the rationale
  depends on them.
- **Internal-structure evidence.** Analyses matching the framework's
  dimensionality claims; for multiple scores, the distinctiveness,
  reliability, and interrelationships of subscores, and the basis for any
  composites. Evidence for separate scores does not by itself justify
  interpreting score differences — see [Reliability of Change
  Scores](/topics/measurement/change-scores).
- **External-relations evidence.** The rationale for each external variable
  and its technical properties; criterion suitability (reliability, construct
  representation, extraneous-variance sources); regression equations or
  conditional criterion information linking score levels to outcome levels,
  not correlations alone; multi-predictor models with cross-validation;
  effect sizes accompanied by standard errors or confidence intervals;
  adjusted statistics reported together with unadjusted values and the
  adjustment procedure.
- **Generalized evidence.** For meta-analytic support: inclusion criteria,
  artifact corrections and their assumptions, moderator analyses, and the
  comparability of the summarized tests, criteria, and situational features to
  the local setting.
- **Consequences.** Evidence for claimed benefits; for classification uses,
  differential treatment outcomes where feasible; and the investigation of any
  unintended consequences' sources.

## Common failure modes

- **"The test is valid."** Unqualified validity claims, or validity treated as
  a property of the instrument rather than of an interpretation for a use in a
  population.
- **Dividing validity into kinds.** Declaring that a test "has content
  validity but not criterion validity," as though these were separate
  properties rather than evidence sources within one argument.
- **The single coefficient.** A lone predictor–criterion correlation offered
  as the whole argument, without scrutiny of criterion quality, score range,
  or the propositions the use actually depends on.
- **Unexamined criteria.** Criterion contamination, unreliability, or
  subgroup bias left unexamined, invalidating the study that rests on them.
- **Design artifacts read as findings.** Extreme-group samples or restricted
  ranges taken to estimate association; attenuation ignored.
- **Consistency coefficients as validity.** An [internal-consistency
  index](/topics/measurement/internal-consistency) or a factor solution offered
  as proof of validity — or of unidimensionality a complex test does not have.
- **Homogeneous review panels.** Content evidence from experts who share the
  test's blind spots, so sources of irrelevant difficulty for subgroups go
  unnoticed.
- **Unsupported transport.** A validated test deployed for a new use,
  population, or modified administration without fresh evidence; meta-analytic
  claims applied where tests, criteria, or settings differ materially.
- **Overread subscores.** Profiles and score differences interpreted without
  evidence for the distinctiveness and reliability of the parts.
- **Differential prediction misdiagnosed.** Subgroup differences in
  test–criterion slopes or intercepts automatically read as test bias — or
  automatically dismissed — without tracing them to test, criterion, or
  measurement error.
- **Consequences misfiled.** Value objections treated as validity refutations,
  or consequences genuinely traceable to underrepresentation and irrelevant
  variance excused as mere policy disputes.
- **Selective reporting.** Adjusted coefficients without their unadjusted
  counterparts, effect sizes without uncertainty, or a literature summary that
  omits inconvenient findings.

Valid interpretation also presupposes the machinery this collection develops
elsewhere: instruments built and documented through the pipeline of [Test
Development](/topics/measurement/test-development) and [Item Development and
Response Formats](/topics/measurement/item-development); item performance
checked in [Classical Item Analysis](/topics/measurement/item-analysis); score
consistency established under [Reliability](/topics/measurement/reliability)
and [Classical Test Theory](/topics/measurement/classical-test-theory); and
association measured and corrected by the tools of
