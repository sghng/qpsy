# Measurement Fundamentals

**Measurement** is the assignment of numerals to objects or events according
to rules, in the classical formulation of
[Stevens (1946)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.103.2684.677). In
psychological measurement the numerals describe *properties* of persons and
their behavior, and the rules are explicit procedures for eliciting, scoring,
and interpreting observations. The definition earns its keep only when each
of its three components is taken separately: the numerals, the properties,
and the rules.

## Numerals, properties, and rules

- **Numerals** are the symbols attached to observations. The kind of numeral
  a rule assigns — a bare category label, a rank, a score with equal units —
  fixes which comparisons the resulting numbers can support; see
  [levels of measurement](#levels-of-measurement).
- **Properties** are the attributes being described. A measurement procedure
  never captures a person wholesale; it targets a property, and everything
  the score means is conditional on that target.
- **Rules** are the procedures that connect observations to numerals. Because
  the rule is the only link between what is observed and what is recorded, a
  score means exactly what its rule stipulates and nothing more.

## Observable properties and latent constructs

Physical attributes — the length of an object, the count of organisms in a
sample — are observable directly, and two competent observers largely agree
on the recorded value. Psychological attributes such as attitudes,
intelligence, or anxiety are **latent constructs**: they cannot be observed
directly, and the degree to which such a construct characterizes a person can
only be inferred from observations of behavior, self-report, or physiology.
No single observation ever absolutely confirms the attribute; every
psychological measurement is an argument from indicators to construct, and
the strength of that argument is what [validity
evidence](/topics/measurement/validity) evaluates.

## Tests and the act of measurement

A **test** is a standard procedure for obtaining a sample of behavior or
performance from a specified domain. Two claims are built into the
definition:

- **Sampling.** A test observes a subset of the domain, not the domain
  itself. The full universe of relevant behavior cannot be enumerated, so
  what the instrument covers — and omits — is a design decision, made when
  the test specifications are written.
- **Standardization.** The same elicitation and scoring procedure applies to
  every respondent; this is the "rule" of the definition made operational.

Measurement of a psychological construct occurs when quantitative values are
assigned to the behavior sample collected with a test: counting endorsed
checklist entries over an observation interval, totaling an inventory, or
timing task performance. The test supplies the observations; the assignment
rule supplies the numbers. How the procedure is designed is the subject of
[Test Development](/topics/measurement/test-development), and how its score
interpretations are supported is the subject of
[Validity](/topics/measurement/validity); the forms individual items take, of
[Item Development and Response Formats](/topics/measurement/item-development).

## Levels of measurement

The kind of numeral a rule assigns defines the **level of measurement**, and
with it the interpretations the scores support
([Stevens (1946)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.103.2684.677)):

| Level | Defining property | Meaningful comparisons | Typical examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominal (categorical) | Identity only; categories carry no order | Same or different category | Group labels, jersey numbers |
| Ordinal | Categories are ordered | Greater or less; rank | Severity grades, ranked lists |
| Interval | Equal differences represent equal amounts | Differences; means | Temperature in °C or °F |
| Ratio | Meaningful zero, so ratios of scores are meaningful | All of the above; ratios | Length, counts, response time |

A score inherits only the properties its rule guarantees. Summing or
averaging scores, computing differences, or forming ratios asserts interval-
or ratio-level structure that ordinal assignments do not carry. Rated
responses make the point concrete: coding ordered verbal categories as
$1, 2, 3, 4$ and averaging the codes presumes that the distance between
adjacent categories is constant — an assumption the categories themselves do
not establish, and one of the recurring problems below.

## Five recurring measurement problems

Every measurement design meets five recurring problems. Each is a standing
risk rather than a one-time obstacle, and each has a dedicated treatment
elsewhere in this collection.

1. **Choosing a method.** No single approach to measuring a construct is
   universally accepted. Direct observation, self-report, teacher report,
   and parent report of the same attribute are not interchangeable, and the
   choice among them is part of what defines the measured attribute.
2. **Sampling behavior.** A test's observations are a limited sample of the
   domain. Generalizing from the sampled items to the construct domain is an
   inference that content planning and item specifications must support.
3. **Random error.** The same person does not behave identically on two
   occasions; repeated measurements disagree for reasons unrelated to the
   construct. The formal treatment is [Classical Test
   Theory](/topics/measurement/classical-test-theory).
4. **Scale properties.** The numerals may not support the arithmetic applied
   to them. Scoring ordered categories as equal units is a working
   assumption — often defensible, never guaranteed by the scale itself; see
   the table above.
5. **Response bias.** Systematic response tendencies, social desirability
   foremost among them, displace scores from the construct they are meant to
   reflect. Detection strategies and remedies are covered in [Response Bias
   and Careless Responding](/topics/measurement/response-bias).

## Assumptions, scope, and failure modes

The rule-based definition makes measurement claim-like: assigning a numeral
asserts that a specific procedure captured a specific property. The standing
failure modes parallel the five problems — a method chosen by convention
instead of argument, content coverage left implicit, transient error
mistaken for real change, arithmetic stronger than the scale supports, and
response bias left unexamined. A sixth, cross-cutting failure is forgetting
that the property measured is latent: scores describe observations, and the
link from observations to construct is itself something to be validated.
